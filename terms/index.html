Terms of art

Terms of art for prevention engineering

The working vocabulary of Mission Zero: names for the delay between knowing and acting, for the hardware at the bed, for making consent reliable, and for what happens after someone asks for help.

David Morris Clayman · Lifesaver Labs PBC · November 18, 2026 · Download as a PDF

Delay and duty

  • Semmelspan: the gap between a preventive insight being published or presented to responsible, knowledgeable parties and institutions acting on it. Semmelspans close in stages, and even the original one remains open: patients still die of infections spread by unwashed hands, in part because of worldwide provider resistance to hand hygiene reliability technology.

  • Horrified Semmelspan: a Semmelspan that people later look back on with horror at how simple the fix was, how long ago it was proposed clearly enough to activate the precautionary principle and warp-speed testing, and how much harm accumulated for lack of traction on sigmoidal community testing and scaling action.

  • Heedlag: the measurable length of that delay.

  • Warnclock and split-times: the clock started at first warning, with each ignored checkpoint marked.

  • Knowdebt: the moral debt that accumulates from knowing and not acting.

  • Seemingly deliberate institutional blindness: an institution’s sustained failure to look at, test or measure a preventable harm it has been repeatedly told about, in a pattern that from the outside looks chosen (anchor: willful blindness, deliberate indifference, institutional betrayal).

  • Lifesavers override: the higher-law principle that when life or grave bodily harm is at stake, the duty to prevent it outranks ordinary procedure, convenience and red tape (anchor: pikuach nefesh, hifz al-nafs, necessity doctrine, emergency exception).

  • Preventable guilt: guilt for a harm that would never have occurred had the responsible institutions met their engineering duty, and which those institutions therefore share (anchor: duty of care, hierarchy of controls).

  • Never Again Engineering: the duty of responsible parties to fund and extend prevention coverage.

  • Counterfactual toll: the harm that has accumulated since bedside prevention technology was first publicly proposed on October 1, 2014, and was not tested.

Hardware and placement

  • Refusal infrastructure: the bedroom equivalent of an andon cord (anchor: stop-work authority, emergency stop).

  • Bedreachable: activatable from the bed either by one touch within arm’s reach, at a standardized headboard spot that can be found in the dark, or by voice, using a short phrase that reaches a wakeable or otherwise voice-activated device (anchor: reach envelope, point-of-care placement, wake-word activation).

  • Mango, grayzone, tag-out and prepanic buttons: the graded button set, each distinguishable by touch.

  • De-vice device and dose-intelligent bedroom: the hardware and the room it creates, one built for privacy and proportional response. An overriding community security imperative puts a touch of legitimate mandatory reporting and review on even the most extreme bedroom privacy models, on the understanding that bedroom violence and coercion patterns spill over to other, later unconsenting parties.

  • Minimal gross-motor activation: usable under freeze, fawn or pinning (anchor: tonic immobility, accessible design).

  • Dispenser-placement analogy: hand-hygiene compliance depends on where the dispenser sits, and the same holds for a safety button.

  • Detertech: deterrence technology standards.

  • Guard state: a mode in which an ordinary cue arms safe-phrase listening.

  • Trigger-utterance button: a bed button that plays a recorded phrase to a second listening device.

  • Universal safeword: “mango mango” for bedrooms, “emergency, emergency” in general.

Consent reliability engineering

  • Consent reliability engineering: the umbrella term for this field (anchor: human factors, safety engineering).

  • Stop codon, readthrough, release factor: a no, a no that gets ignored, and the fast exit mechanism. Modeled on how cells make stop signals reliable: a strong, conserved, almost unmisinterpretable signal, a favorable surrounding context, release factors close at hand, and error-checking mechanisms. “No means no” offers the opposite, a refusal that requires interpretation and explanation in hot state, may be stated as little as once, and uses a word used in wobbly, often arguable or negotiable ways in hundreds of other contexts, which leaves immediate confusion about its specificity, scope and finality. Compare “Stop, Drop, and Roll” or the bedroom version, “Stop, Dress, and Roll,” each of which pairs a distinctive phrase with trained motor coordination; “No means no” never attempted even a small step toward clarity, such as “Bedroom no means no no no, triple full-scope no, get dressed.”

  • Cold state, hot state, high voltage: calm deliberation, arousal, and the intimate activity during which judgment is under greatest load (anchor: hot-cold empathy gap).

  • Counterpotential theory and Ulysses Pact counterpotential: a drilled, visible button keeps cold-state ethics stable in hot state (anchor: precommitment device).

  • Interruption and interruptibility leverage: how easily a stubborn or coercive course can be stopped mid-way.

  • Affirmative consent envelopes: periodic, music-coordinated reaffirmation, with affirmation window, lapse, check-in, alarm.

  • FRIES SAVER: FRIES plus Staged, Alarmable, Verified, Expiring, Responsive.

  • Ground-rule package: boundaries agreed when cold, with neck pressure never included.

  • Copilot Consent (or “Cornell Consent”): a handclasp or arm clasp exchanged before high voltage, in which each partner promises an enthusiastic yes and promises to pause or halt on request. Consent strength is signaled by squeeze count, which each partner can read by feel in the dark: three squeezes for a full yes (“yes, yes, yes”), fewer for hesitation, and none or a release for no.

  • Stop, Dress, and Roll: the separation choreography after a no.

  • Harm manifold: contraception, STIs, ground rules and assault treated together.

  • Sensation gap: neither partner can feel the other’s pleasure, pain or fright.

  • Consent speeding and high-voltage precaution failure: the lesser-offense categories.

  • BAC consent threshold: a defined alcohol limit for first-time consent. To our knowledge, no U.S. state, and no country, state or locality anywhere in the world, has defined one, even though intoxication and consent are litigated daily in courts around the world. Nor has any jurisdiction set different thresholds for new and established relationships, if that distinction is needed for community acceptance. We recommend 0.04 BAC for new relationships, borrowing the FAA’s limit for pilots, on the reasoning that a first encounter is the operation of high-voltage, heavy, too frequently unsafe machinery. An alternative is 0.05, Utah’s per se limit for impaired driving, the strictest of any U.S. state and the only one to follow the National Transportation Safety Board’s evidence-based 2013 recommendation, which industry groups opposed. For most adults either figure means one or two drinks at most, after which a “duty to know” one’s own BAC and one’s partner’s attaches.

    Past assaults show the stakes. Roughly half of sexual assaults studied involved drinking by the perpetrator, the victim or both [1]. Among college rape victims surveyed nationally in 2015, three quarters reported being incapacitated by alcohol at the time, compared with half in the mid-1980s [2]. Incapacitation lies far beyond 0.04 or 0.05, so at least that share of those assaults happened above this threshold. The true share has never been measured, because BAC is almost never recorded.

    Testing is easy: once a venue has a breath tester, each test costs about as much as a wrapped plastic straw, the price of a disposable mouthpiece. Free breath testing should be built into the price of every alcoholic drink sold at any liquor-licensed premises.

  • Cancerol, crasherol, raperol: harm-named labels for alcohol, for use at any dose, or anywhere alcohol is served or drunk and BAC can’t be measured on the spot, free and without frustration.

  • Calibration: a proposed one-time supervised teaching session whose purpose is dissuasion and self-awareness, held under a one-time legal exception to the minimum drinking age. Opted-in upperclass high school students, with parental consent, drink measured doses of alcohol, presented under the cancerol, crasherol and raperol names, while being taught to recognize how each BAC level impairs judgment and motor coordination. The session teaches the current standards that physicians and health workers have been aligning on, not the weaker ones the alcohol industry has pressed governments to adopt, and it is designed to discourage students from ever exceeding them. Doses stop at whatever level is ethically reasonable to test once, and never above the state’s adult legal driving limit, which is 0.08 everywhere but Utah. A state that wants calibration to stop lower can lower its limit. In the meantime the ritual may help students:

    • deromanticize drunkenness;

    • learn the point at which their own motor coordination and judgment begin to fail;

    • appreciate that alcohol’s cancer risk begins at low doses;

    • practice measuring BAC quickly, with digital elimination-curve tools they can come to rely on once testing is built into serving culture and alcohol sales, so they can judge the BAC consent threshold strictly in future relationships, new or standing;

    • practice resisting peer pressure to drink, on the principle that non-carcinogenic “focohol” alternatives should always be on offer, free of bullying or undue inducement;

    • connect the next day’s hangover to the previous night’s dose as its direct and painful cost.

  • Duo mode and Solo mode: both partners enrolled, or one person running the system as a discreet protective layer. Duo mode is strongly preferred. It bundles far more harm prevention, including the chance to talk through STI and pregnancy prevention together with computational precision and reliability logic support. But it depends on institutional sponsorship and cultural change. Few men or women will risk unsettling or losing a partner by being the one to raise a consent protocol or consent technology. An institution has to normalize it first, on all sides, as an ordinary part of a healthy, do-no-harm match of the quality people look for in a marriage partner.

Reporting and response

  • Gray state: unease the person isn’t ready to classify.

  • Mango color range: mistaken green, gray, yellow, orange, red.

  • Grayzone reporting and triage: taking in and sorting reports that aren’t black and white.

  • Consent Primary Response (Bedroom CPR): the trained first response to a bedroom alert, given by nearby peers in the minutes before any professional arrives, on the model of bystander CPR. A button press or safeword sends a Neighbor 911 dispatch to opted-in responders within a short sprint. They knock, separate the two people, check on each one privately, and stay until both are safe or the alert is escalated. Their job is to interrupt and stabilize; investigating and judging belong to others. Neighbor 911 supplies the capability (dispatch, proximity matching, a trained and drilled responder roster). Responders who opt in accept the duty to go when dispatched, follow the script, and hand off to campus security or emergency services when an alert stays red (anchor: bystander CPR, chain of survival, active bystander intervention, Good Samaritan law).

  • Peer door-knock response, separate SES questioning, next-day check-in: the three response steps after a press.

  • Mild, firm, forceful treatment: the three response levels, chosen by the reporting party (the innocenter) via a judgment direction. Institutions should exercise epistemic humility about events they did not witness, and should trust reporting parties to keep the process from overrunning the punitive or threat range they want. That trust, together with adjudication in a fortnight or less, is what makes reporting possible without disrupting and unraveling anyone’s educational or professional momentum. The large majority of incidents go unreported, in part from fear that a report will trigger a draconian, automatic sledgehammer against a peer, which the reporting party cannot stand down once it is out of proportion. Many people who would otherwise report a gray, yellow or red stay silent because they cannot trust that the person they name won’t be treated as a hardened, deliberate criminal. Some cases will by law require forceful treatment, including arrest and detention. Many others, especially first-time consent violations, call for a dredgeable record combined with honesty, candor, apology, a firm warning, probation, and a thorough review of careful, conscientious, hot-state-stable, affirmative enthusiastic consent practice, supported by the precautionary guardrails of the high-voltage safety technology standards disclosed here. The deterrent is statistical confidence: any further violation will almost certainly come to review and be handled more harshly, because the next reporting party will see that a dredged record exists.

  • Fortnight, Fair, Horcruxed, Dualindiv Hearing: the hearing standard.

    • Fortnight: held and decided within two weeks of the report.

    • Fair: both parties know the complaint, see the evidence and are heard by a neutral panel.

    • Horcruxed: each party holds a fragment of the shared memory of what happened. The fragments can only be assembled consistently, with the fewest gaps and misreadings of what each perceived, when both memories are present and available in the room with no partition between them (see the CAP theorem). The truth can only be reconstructed with both accounts in the room.

    • Dualindiv: each party is heard individually first, then the two accounts are examined together, with the aim of mutual understanding, apology where owed, and closure. The word embraces our interpersonally constructed identities. As social beings we show different behavior, circuitry, memories and epitopes of soul depending on who we are with, so no one is best studied in solitary confinement, apart from the only other witness. The idea is old. The full range of confrontation must be open to students, as they wish, must and can: from warm agreement to open argument and attacks on character, within the limits seen in couples counseling at one end and a divorce courtroom at the other. Without it, a panel cannot judge social and individual character when moral questions are in dispute.

      No one can evade the duty to bear witness. A party who is being gaslit must see it in the hearing and have the fortitude to say so. Fear of being traumatized by facing the other person does not excuse attendance, because the alternative is a world in which the accused can spin a defense unchallenged before the listeners who must decide whether to change a life to protect others. Presence also lets the accused point out what the reporting party may be leaving out; a panel cannot assume any account is complete or fully honest. New questions open in a hearing that no written statement anticipated, and a voice absent in that instant is not weighed, with outcomes that can be badly skewed. An institution that persuades reporting parties they are not strong enough to attend does them and the accused a disservice. Absence also traps the honest. A generally good person may sacrifice their own defense to keep the reporting party’s privacy. Had the reporting party been present, the two could have gently agreed, together, on what needed to be said: private experiences they had shared and once agreed to keep symmetrically private, which bear on the judgment of the case and on how much care and precaution each exercised over time. The graver risk lies in absence: a generally good person condemned without the one conversation that could anchor the truth, in a proceeding that becomes a Star Chamber (anchor: Confrontation Clause, due process, live hearing, restorative justice conference).

  • Disclosure trap: a student is bound to disclose a disciplinary outcome to later schools and employers, while the institution that produced the outcome refuses to say what must be disclosed, gives contradictory answers through different offices, and offers no appeal to resolve them (anchor: institutional action disclosure).

  • Disclosure trilemma: the choice an unresolved disciplinary record forces on every later application: disclose a rejected allegation, deny it and risk a false statement, or stay silent and be penalized (anchor: the “cruel trilemma” of self-accusation, perjury or contempt in Fifth Amendment law).

  • Sealed-letter penalty: the confidential letters an academic program sends to admissions bodies about a student, which the student cannot inspect, trace or answer. With no review or inspection process, the letters are an avenue for blacklisting and for unchecked leverage over the student. They are terrifying in cases like these, where accusatory and untrustworthy communication continues even after a hearing panel has found the student not responsible on every charge: the student cannot learn what is being said, by whom, or to which schools.

  • National Bill of Student Rights and Rites: a common national standard for student disciplinary systems. It would establish a civil rights and ethics board with power to inspect the confidential letters schools send about students and to report on unfounded, potentially life-altering discrepancies in them.

  • Sealed-letter review board: an external civil rights and ethics body, independent of every school whose letters it reviews, created under the Bill. It inspects confidential letters shuttled between schools, which today amount to unreviewable gossip about students, and reports any statement lacking an adequate evidentiary basis. Its disclosure rule has two tiers:

    • Full notice to the student of anything describing bad character or conduct, any incriminating information, and any deep attack on character, with a right to respond and to have unsupported statements corrected or withdrawn. Any persistent, forwardable disciplinary record falls in this category.

    • Continued confidentiality for ordinary evaluative judgments, such as where an evaluator ranks the student among all those they have taught. Routine disclosure of these would push evaluators to inflate them. The board looks behind one only on cause for suspicion: that it is out of keeping with the student’s file and amounts to an unmerited blacklisting surprise.

    The board also rules on timing. For each kind of record it decides what must be disclosed up front, where it may fairly serve as a red flag that ends an application without further thought, and what may be disclosed only after the school has made a preliminary decision on admissibility. Deferred disclosure isolates a single question: whether the student’s disciplinary record is justly fatal to membership in the community she or he seeks to enter, or something that can be accommodated, perhaps on probationary good-conduct terms in the first year.

    An example of the second kind is an incoming medical or nursing student found responsible, in the fourth year of college, for smoking tobacco, vaping or binge drinking. The conduct is legal, though arguably it should not be. It is also medically imprudent, an addiction our culture tolerates despite its death toll, and it shows poor judgment and ethics in a future healthcare professional. Whether it is permanently disqualifying is a judgment call for the admitting institution. Because every other factor has already been decided, the institution can admit, admit on probationary terms, or refuse on that one ground, and a student who is refused or put on probation can take that single, isolated question to court if they believe they must and can win.

    In a contested case between two students, the minimum basis for any finding of fact is an observed discussion or debate between them, an obvious hygiene step (anchor: inspector general, ombudsman, credit-report dispute rights, fair-chance hiring).

  • Dredgeable records: private yellows that survive and can be matched later (anchor: Callisto-style matching escrow).

  • Conflict recording: sealed, evidence-chained and optional.

  • Apology and candor rites and the held-apology channel: structured ways to apologize, including during a no-contact order.

  • Impulsive middle: the zone between clear consent and clear assault, where most preventable events happen.

  • Interrupt side and refusal side: the two halves of co-design, divided by expertise. Engineers who never want to cause harm again lead on what a signal must look, sound and feel like to stop someone mid-course. Survivors lead on placement, effort and what follows a press, and hold a veto there.

Harm vocabulary

  • Strangulation incidence: how often choking, strangling or neck pressure occurs in campus sexual encounters. Non-fatal strangulation can cause hypoxic brain injury, a deadening of the capacity to learn that is lethally inconsistent with a university’s mission.

  • Gentle, pushy, coercive or stubborn, assault: a four-step public vocabulary for the range of sexual conduct.

  • Ape-rape spectrum and apist: a proposed shorthand for the same range, from primitive and coercive to civilized and consensual, covering conduct short of rape. “Apist” is an intermediate label, milder than “rapist,” meant to lower the threshold for naming harm. The term is meant species-wide, since all humans are great apes, and carries no racial meaning. Because the word “ape” has a racist history, the term is offered as an open question, with an invitation to African-American students, scholars and community members to test whether it can be reclaimed race-neutrally.

Argument: the speed of justice is a prevention lever

A misconduct report lands on both people like an anvil on their attention. Coursework, career and reputation all come to hang on an outcome that hasn’t arrived, and nothing else gets a fair share of the mind until the matter is heard and settled. Imagine attending classes for a month with that pending. Imagine rehearsing one night’s memories for half a year so you can testify accurately.

That wait is normal today. Louisiana’s university systems report averages of 45 to 90 days, with LSU running as long as 222 [3]. Michigan State averaged nearly 190 days in 2018 [4]. One Stanford proceeding ran past 600 days, and a researcher of campus sexual violence quoted in the same report calls delay “the number one complaint from survivors” [5].

Much of the trauma blamed on the incident is caused by the wait. A fortnight standard treats time as a cost charged to the innocenter and the innocentest alike, and requires a conclusive investigation and hearing before the case metastasizes into a wrecked semester or career.

Speed alone is not enough. Each party holds part of the memory of what happened. Separate interviews relayed through administrators produce two accounts that never touch, exposed to Prisoner’s Dilemma, Telephone Game and man-in-the-middle distortion.

No panel can claim a preponderance of evidence when its two main sources have never been tested against each other, each reacting to the epitopes of the other’s memories and moral choices, on questions such as mutual privacy and even-handed candor. Think of a classic he-said, she-said case in thirds. Each party interviewed alone supplies one third of the necessary evidence. The final third exists only in their interaction, assembled in real time: how they converse and debate, how they sort what matters from what doesn’t, and where the pivot points lie that each can discuss with no one else.

Privacy makes that third irreplaceable. Two people who dated may be bound by promises to each other, by matters already forgiven, or by religious or civic rules against gossip. Neither knows how much to say to a panel, or whether saying it is permitted, unless the other is in the room to show face to face that it is consistent with their own privacy and candor choices. With both present, each can affirm, deny, steady or correct the record as it forms, and object when the other strays from credible truth.

Separation also punishes honesty. A reporting party cannot be assumed never to have written or spoken in a misleading, self-protective way. A reported party who is completely candid and does not protect himself in kind can be destroyed by that asymmetry when the two accounts never meet. Institutions have an incentive to keep them apart, since a divided record lowers lawsuit risk, and the result is a telephone game presented as “shuttle diplomacy” by an intermediary who may not understand what happened.

The stakes are life-altering. A panel’s findings of fact, sometimes issued with little or no meaningful appeal after a hearing the reporting party did not attend, can block a student from law school, medical school, or a public or national security career. The mechanism is compulsory disclosure. Ethics, religion, contract and professional rules oblige the student to disclose the outcome to later admissions bodies, yet the institution declines to define what must be disclosed or to answer for it.

The trap closes when an outcome is mixed. A panel may clear a student while an administrator states that he should have been found responsible. A decision may reject every charge and still include a stray adverse remark about his judgment. Different offices of the same university may then give opposite answers on whether any of it must be disclosed, the outside bodies that receive disclosures defer to the institution, and no internal appeal exists to settle the question.

The student then faces a disclosure trilemma on every future application:

  1. Self-incrimination. Disclose, and report as a standing official allegation something the panel rejected, because one officer put their own judgment above the panel’s and no one above them will resolve it.

  2. False statement. Answer no under signature or oath, though a ranking officer has said disclosure may be required and has never been overruled in writing.

  3. Silence. Decline to answer, and be penalized anyway for a doubt the student did not create. The penalty travels in sealed, confidential letters that the academic program sends to admissions bodies and that the student can never inspect. Those letters can carry an officer’s own post-hearing impression, formed at a hearing where that same officer arranged for the two parties never to speak. The officer’s confidence was never tested: the reporting party was not there to concede a point or to stand by the accusation, so the officer had nothing to rely on when doubting the panel’s collective judgment and the student’s account.

The only exits are a public lawsuit that makes the episode permanent, usually without a lawyer, or watching one’s goals and path collapse. A system that refuses the one conversation capable of settling right and wrong, then leaves a person in that bind for years, can drive people to despair and, for some, to suicidal crisis.

Much of this is preventable if the parties simply talk. A joint hearing on a complete record lets a student answer the one remark that ends up mattering, even if he agrees in part and disagrees in part. A fuller, less structurally warped truth emerges when everyone responsible is in the room together: for candor, for prevention, for supporting truth-telling, and for deciding temporary or lifelong consequences. That is community “con-science,” moral science done together.

So the rule: within two weeks of a report, the reporting party and the reported party take part in a structured conversation before the panel. A reporting party who will not bear witness has the complaint dismissed.

A personal account

I was punched about a dozen times down a street in Lisbon. As far as I know I did nothing to provoke it. I had gone into a bar to wait for the police, who were coming to take my report of a theft down the street, and told a server I would probably just order a drink. When I asked the manager what time the bar closed, he wouldn’t say at first. Then he claimed it was closing at 10 PM, six minutes away, and that I couldn’t be seated or served. It was a shady maneuver. I suspected that making me leave on those grounds was not lawful, any more than I believed it would be in the United States, so I stood still to check whether the bar really closed at 2 AM. Before I could find out even the closing time, he shoved me out the door so forcefully that I fell to the ground outside, twisting my knee in the fall. When I got up and asked why, he started punching. He hit me again and again, with a trained street fighter’s aggression, while I did nothing but ask why he was attacking me and why he wouldn’t stop. Punches and two kicks drove me backward about 50 meters (160 feet) down the street, and I needed an MRI at a hospital to check for a brain bleed. He acted as if he owned the street and I could never return to his neighborhood. I walked back up the hill and called the police.

Thank heavens it was Portugal, where we have national healthcare. Had I had my bell rung that hard in the United States, I would have been too frightened of the opaque and punishing cost of a hospital admission and an MRI to go in at all. I would have treated it like a bad boxing bout and gone without medical supervision, because American healthcare finance is so atrociously expensive, untransparent and fearfully slow to settle. I’m glad I was assaulted in Portugal and not in my hometown of Boca Raton, Florida, where prices are hidden for months when our computing systems could quote a capped, insurance-adjusted price in the instant before care. Patients are instead bound to pay costs they agreed to under duress and in manufactured ignorance, and can be labeled “noncompliant” for asking what a service costs.

The police never told me when I could be present to hear his account, or to speak with him before a judge, prosecutor or jury, under a court’s power and responsibility to guide two Portuguese citizens, him and me, toward a lifetime of good conduct and nonviolence. Without that encounter I have no stable theory of why he did it or of what punishment would be right. I don’t know how we would have spoken to each other, how I might have helped the court turn him permanently from violence, or how meeting might have changed his ideas and mine about citizenship, a proprietor’s ethics and community justice. I don’t even know whether he was punished or whether the case was dismissed because I wasn’t there, though I asked repeatedly for updates and offered to fly back to Portugal to testify.
