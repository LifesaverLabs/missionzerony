A statewide goal for New York

Make the first stop the last one anyone needs.

Mission Zero is a plan to drive every kind of sexual assault in New York toward zero, with a special focus on date and acquaintance rape and other sexual coercion, even in the gray zone. It isn't only about college. That risk follows everyone who dates, from senior prom to a first marriage before or after 30, and beyond it: working adults, people who never went to college, and New Yorkers dating again at 18 or 81. It treats assault the way we treat fire: with prevention built into rooms, phones, parties and law, tested in a statewide trial and measured honestly.

Available now

Three plans, bundled in every bedroom

This isn't a menu to pick from. Everyone should have access to all three, Plan A, Plan B and Plan C, in every bedroom where people are sexually active before marriage, and, given the long history of marital rape, in many marital bedrooms too. Bundled together, they give people several ways to call for a pause or for help, whatever state they're in and whatever their stress response:

  • When they can speak, a safeword works even with hands held or a phone out of reach (Plan B).
  • When they freeze, can't find words, or speaking feels unsafe, a silent press of a button within reach still works (Plan C).
  • When fear, alcohol or the urge to appease makes any signal hard, rehearsed habits do the work: partners trained to ask first and to stop at silence, and reflexes practiced until they're automatic (Plan A), backed by systems that notice when affirmation goes quiet (Plan B).

No single option fits every person or every moment. Fight, flight, freeze and fawn each close off some ways of asking for help, so the bedroom should keep the others open.

Plan A isn't enough on its own. Education and training work best when they're anchored in the guaranteed availability of Plan B or Plan C. Knowing that anyone who feels pressured can call for help in an instant if their wishes aren't respected changes behavior before anything goes wrong. It pushes both partners to take more care and attention to get affirmative, enthusiastic consent right from the start and to keep checking as things go on. That protects everyone's innocence, including the person who would never want to cross a line but might misread a moment.

A

Plan A: Better education and psychomotor training

Teach ask-first norms and rehearse the motions until they're automatic: an ask-first handshake before a first kiss, a clear hand signal for later stages, a universal safeword practiced like stop, drop and roll, and evidence-based resistance and bystander training.

No devices · Low cost · P-19, P-20, P-27

B

Plan B: Privacy-focused, safeword-sensitive systems

Phones, watches and speakers people already own coordinate, coach, referee or alert when someone says the safeword, with anything from minimal to full-strength processing that stays on the device. Nothing is recorded or sent unless someone asks for help.

Devices already owned · Choose a privacy level · P-76, P-79

C

Plan C: Gray-zone and red-zone prepanic buttons

A button in the same spot at every headboard. One press for confusion or a gray-zone moment starts a quiet check-in; a red-zone press calls for help. No microphone, no camera, and pressing is always a choice. Call it a prepanic button or a gray-zone button: it's meant to be pressed before panic sets in, just as much as after.

About $20 a button · Try it above · P-43, P-77

The wrong tree

Barking up the wrong tree

The problem

The system notices too late

Most sexual assaults are not attacks by strangers. They happen between people who know each other, on dates, after parties, in bedrooms, on and off campus. We respond with rape kits, investigations and hearings. All of it starts after the harm. And reporting is rare enough that one person can harm several others before anyone files a report.

Everyone who dates. Campuses get the headlines, but most people at risk aren't students. The risk of date rape and sexual coercion, including gray-zone pressure, starts for many around senior prom and lasts through every stretch of single life: the twenties after school or instead of it, dating again after a divorce or a death, and new relationships at 50, 60 or 81. A bartender in Buffalo, a nurse on night shifts in the Bronx and a retiree on a dating app in Albany deserve the same protection as a sophomore in a dorm. Whether or not someone can afford college, wants it, or has long since finished it, they deserve a minimal bedroom safety standard.

At the 20% rate at which college women report rape and sexual assault to police, someone must harm about 3 people before any report is more likely than not, and about 10 before one is nearly certain (Bureau of Justice Statistics, 1995–2013). Gray-zone pressure, such as persisting after a no, is reported far less, and no one measures it, so the lower rows below are illustrations.

The design gap

Bedrooms were never designed for the harm that happens in them

A dorm room, a studio apartment or a hotel room comes with a smoke detector, a marked fire exit and often a sprinkler head and a carbon monoxide alarm. It comes with nothing for sexual assault, even though more than half of sexual assaults happen at or near the victim's home (RAINN). Whoever designed these rooms made a design failure: they engineered them for fire and left out the harm so many people who date fear most. The people who design a room's furniture, surfaces and standard devices bear responsibility for what those things could have prevented and didn't. Design failures can be fixed.

The technology isn't new. Building intercoms were patented in 1894, and two-way intercoms were common in apartment buildings by the 1950s. Hospitals have wired call buttons at every bed, and affordable hardwired panic buttons, which could be reframed as prepanic buttons for the gray zone or the red zone, have been ordinary parts of security systems for decades. That dorms, apartments and other buildings weren't built around this kind of safety was a choice: a choice to neglect one of the greatest safety risks people face from their teens through every later season of dating. Someday we need a historical examination of exactly why bedrooms were never engineered to protect women and men from assaults that go uninterrupted because a person freezes, goes still or fawns.

I've been Semmelweissing this since 2015. Like Ignaz Semmelweis, who showed in the 1840s that handwashing saved mothers' lives and was ignored for years, I've argued on and off since 2015 for building sexual assault safety into bedrooms. Despite my best efforts, the message hasn't changed campus housing standards, let alone the standards for apartments, rentals and hotels where most adults sleep. Perhaps after the uproar over the Cornell case, campus housing authorities, landlords, builders and those who influence them will finally build bedrooms with clear, direct safety features for sexual assault prevention, using modern electronics that are privacy-engineered and worthy of people's trust.

Part of the reason may be fear of how it looks. A college that builds a help button into its bedrooms worries that parents will read it as an admission that its dorms are dangerous. I once met a parent who took one campus's gift of a personal emergency bracelet to every incoming student as a sign that the campus was especially dangerous, when it was really a sign that the campus was especially careful.

The answer is for every campus to move at once. When New Jersey required sprinklers in every dorm after the 2000 Seton Hall fire, no campus looked dangerous for having them. If every New York campus adopts the same housing safety standard at the same time, with unobtrusive, privacy-first technology in the same spot in every bedroom, it stops looking like a confession and becomes a baseline, like a seatbelt in every car. Then parents and visiting high school students will ask why a campus doesn't have it yet. The same logic holds for landlords, hotels and home builders: when the standard is statewide, renters and guests will start asking why a bedroom doesn't have it. The proposals for this are P-39, P-41, P-43 and P-77.

A device is a chance to de-vice. Every device in a bedroom, from a speaker to a lock to a headboard, is a chance to reduce vice: coercion, pressure and harm. The goal is to diminish vice, not sex. If anything, safe sexuality should grow when people feel protected enough to express themselves and explore within safe bounds, under ground rules both partners understand, in caring and loving relationships.

For every couple. Mission Zero is pro-LGBTQ. Every tool and proposal works the same for partners of any gender or orientation, never requires anyone to reveal their orientation to anyone else, and treats every consenting relationship equally. CDC surveys find that lesbian, gay and bisexual people face sexual violence at higher rates than their straight peers, so they stand to gain the most from rooms built to protect everyone.

    I serve in first-year student government at the University of Chicago, and miss what mattered most

    Semmelspan: about 23 years

    As a first-year member of student government, I did no significant work or advocacy on sexual assault prevention. I didn’t adequately realize how serious, pervasive and preventable the problem was, though I certainly should have. It was then, and always has been, by far the greatest safety threat in any university community in North America, and it should have been my number one priority. Instead I spent time on all-night library access during finals week, the campus meal plan and dozens of other far less important issues.

    That year I also took part in a design review session for the South Campus Residence Hall, as the University was designing it and seeking community acceptance of its design standards. It opened in 2009 with 811 student beds. They asked what first-year students needed in it. I was an idiot: I failed to think about or demand a layer of safety built into the beds, bedside tables or walls for all the women and men who would live there, such as standardized gray-zone “confusion or prepanic” buttons, when I could have. The University could have tested it there. If sexual assault rates in that dorm proved lower, or if the theory was accepted as strong enough on its own (this was the University of Chicago, after all), it could have retrofitted the rest of the housing in and around campus with matching fixtures or fixed devices that balance privacy and safety.

    My entering class, the Class of 2007, had 1,172 first-year students. Applying the University of Chicago’s own 2019 campus climate survey rates, the closest measure available, and with 602 women and 553 men entering as first-time freshmen: about 175 of my classmates were likely sexually assaulted during college, by physical force or while unable to consent, about 80 of them by rape or attempted rape, and about 70 in our first year alone. I did barely anything to help them, or to make sure it would never happen again to the classes that came after us. As a member of student government, I had a duty to protect my peers, and I fell far short of it. I was part of the problem.

A different night

How a night could go differently

Here is how a date or party night could unfold with systems that can be built and adopted today. Campuses are the easiest place to test them, but the same layers work for anyone dating, in apartments, hotels and homes anywhere in New York. Each step is a layer; none depends on the person at risk recognizing danger in time.

At the door

A registered party runs breath tests at entry and marks served guests, as bars already do with wristbands. Free drink-test strips sit at every bar. Above a set breath-test line no one can consent, and in a group encounter that bars everyone in the room.

Arriving somewhere unfamiliar

If someone arrives late at night at a residence their phone has never seen, after drinking or with a new date, the phone raises one quiet, private check-in: fine, "check on me," or help. Nothing leaves the phone unless they ask.

Before anything starts

If either partner may have had more than one drink for a woman, or two for a man, each has a duty to know the BAC picture: the instant reading and what it means, whether each can consent right now, and if not, when they will be able to. A breath sensor beside the headboard button shows all three, because blood alcohol keeps rising after the last drink. Without a test, the answer is to wait.

At the first "stop"

A press of the headboard button, or a universal safeword spoken aloud and trained as reflexively as stop, drop and roll, sets three things off within seconds. The phone or speaker, listening on the device for that one word and nothing else, stops the music and plays a calm check-in. A silent alert with the room number goes to a trained, sober responder nearby: a peer on campus, a vetted neighbor or a trusted contact anywhere else. If there's no answer within a minute, campus safety or 911 gets the room-level location too.

Minutes later

A responder knocks: "Welfare check." A knock can end an incident without anyone being arrested, and it arrives while harm can still be stopped. If the person at risk wants to leave, a refuge for the night is assigned in minutes: a lockable safe-sleep room or a vetted host, opened with a time-limited key, never revealed to the person left behind.

The morning after

Every attendee gets one optional, anonymous "how was last night?" message. A survivor can lock a time-stamped account in a private evidence vault, and file a sealed name that unlocks only if someone else names the same person.

The pilot

The first statewide Mission Zero trial

New York would be the first state to run Mission Zero statewide: a three-year, campus-randomized trial across participating colleges and training programs, testing which combinations of policy, environment and technology cut sexual assault the most per dollar. Each arm adds a layer to the one before, so the state sees what each added step buys. Its size follows what New York chooses to include, and a focused start can be inexpensive.

Campuses are where the trial starts, not where the problem ends. Schools can randomize, survey and measure in ways the wider public can't, so they make the cleanest first test. What works there is meant to reach everyone who dates: the same button, safeword and check-ins in rentals, hotels, homes and dating apps, for New Yorkers who never enrolled or graduated decades ago.

Privacy

A slightly different balance, with hard guarantees

This asks people who date to accept a button above every bed and a phone that listens for one word. We accepted smoke detectors in every bedroom because they do one job and report nothing else. Safety tech for the bedroom has to meet that bar, in law and in code.

  • Detection happens on the device, including reading the affirmative signals of the consent envelope. Nothing is recorded or sent unless the person triggers it.
  • Open standards with several certified vendors, so no single company controls the system.
  • Published data flows and verifiable builds, so anyone can check the software does only what it says.
  • Pressing a button or using any tool is always the person's choice. Declining carries no penalty and no adverse inference.
  • A record of a yes from any tool can never be used as proof of consent.
  • No cameras and no brain-signal data in intimate-safety devices.
  • Records stay in the user's custody, encrypted to keys only they hold.
  • Statistics leave a campus, building or app only as de-identified totals, in groups large enough that no one can be identified.

Many designs, all testable. The consent envelope isn't one product. It is a family of configurations that can be designed and tested in the pilot, each set by the people using it:

  • Safeword only: listens for nothing but the universal safeword.
  • Cue words: also listens for a small, preprogrammed set of words the partners chose, such as go, pause, slow down and stop.
  • Deeper reading: a private on-device model interprets how things are going from how partners interact, never from body signals alone.
  • Private intimacy coordinator: at the far end, an electronic intimacy coordinator you can talk to beforehand to scope out what you want and don't want, which then supports you to the max during the moment, all on the device.

How the envelope carries consent: the music's loudness follows the quietest partner's affirmations.

Levels 2 to 6 use patent-pending mechanisms, licensed on low-cost, fair terms (see the pledge). Every level keeps the button and the safe phrase, and stop always wins.

Already here

Tools on the market or coming soon

Much of a Mission Zero setup can be assembled today from products other companies already sell. Every setup should offer both a touch path and a voice path: voice works when hands are held or a phone is out of reach, and touch works when speaking is unsafe. Mission Zero's standards are open, so any of these can be certified and used.

No new hardware required. Every piece can be off the shelf: phones, watches, smart speakers, programmable buttons and breath sensors that are already sold today. New hardware engineering is the slowest and hardest part of any safety system, harder than software and platform integration, and Mission Zero doesn't depend on it. What holds this back is focus: a community spotlight, and intense cooperation among industry and institutions, both collaborative and competitive, aimed at the Mission Zero goal. It takes a willingness to take responsibility for the engineering fixes, and to withstand sometimes wild public criticism and early rejection while public understanding matures, until everyone is comfortable with minimally invasive, privacy-centered "bedroom seatbelt" technology for high-voltage moments across the hot-cold empathy gap.

Why platforms wait. The first-mover problem that keeps one campus from adding bedroom safety features also holds back the biggest platforms. A company like Amazon may not want to be the one to spearhead a universal, freely available bedroom safeword on every Alexa device owned by Prime members, possibly for fear of an unpredictable customer backlash or boycott spilling over onto its core shopping business. No company wants to move alone on this, and no individual inventor or small business can make a platform prioritize options to prevent sexual assault in Prime members' bedrooms, students or not, however clearly the need and the capacity are laid out. That changes when Prime members, institutions, the public and the New York State Assembly ask for it together.

The tools

Built for the moments outside the session

Most safety tools wait for a person to recognize danger and act. These are designed for the moments where that fails: before a decision is made in the heat of the moment, at the point of exit, during a frozen dispute, and in the bedroom itself. Each is patent pending and will be licensed on low-cost, fair terms so that patents never block adoption (see the pledge).

In the bedroom, by choice · Patent pending

An affirmative consent envelope

For partners who opt in, often early in a relationship, music plays only while each person keeps giving a simple sign that things are going well: a tap, a squeeze, or a word they enrolled. If the signs stop, the music fades and a private check-in follows. Consent becomes something the bedroom helps people check on, instead of something assumed to be on autopilot.

That matters because some of the most common reactions to pushed boundaries are quietness and tonic immobility, the involuntary freeze many survivors describe. The wish to stop often forms long before a person says so, and then they may say it softly. A fading song notices the silence. This asks for more trust than the guard state, because devices have to read affirmative, enthusiastic signals, so that processing stays on the device and nothing is recorded or sent. Body signals never count as a yes, either partner can privately drop the envelope as trust grows, and the safe phrase stays armed throughout.

In the bedroom · Patent pending

A guard state armed by an ordinary cue

A song, a routine voice request or a normal device command quietly arms the room's devices, with no visible change. They listen on the device, without a wake word, only for a safe phrase, and keep no other audio. A safe phrase starts a private check-in, then an alarm, then a call for help, each only if there's no answer. False alarms are filtered before anyone calls 911, and any request for emergency help carries a confidence level.

On arrival · Patent pending

A late-night arrival check-in

Apple's and Google's safety check-ins confirm you got home and treat arrival as the end of risk. This one is raised by arrival itself: late at night, at an unfamiliar residence, in a risky context. It's private, dismissible in one gesture, and has a duress answer that looks like an ordinary dismissal.

At the point of exit · Patent pending

A refuge for the night

Someone who wants to leave a partner's or host's room at 2 a.m. often has nowhere safe to go. A discreet request, by button, check-in answer or phrase, gets screened for medical risk first, then matched to a safe-sleep room or a vetted host with no possibility of romantic interest, and opened with a time-limited credential.

After a report · Patent pending

A held-apology and clarification channel

A no-contact order protects the complainant but freezes every misunderstanding in place. Through an ombudsperson, or any office entrusted with the role, a restrained party can submit an apology held for the protected party to open only if they choose, and ask closed, structured questions about the investigator's understanding. Each side can verify what was transmitted, changed or withheld, without relying on the intermediary's word.

During a no-contact order · Patent pending

Distance without surveillance

Phones that hold a shared secret can tell when the two parties are about to cross paths, by Bluetooth or private location matching, and gently suggest a different route, venue or time. Neither learns where the other is. Class and work calendars forecast conflicts early, and both can periodically ask for more distance or less.

Everywhere · Patent pending

One universal safeword

New York adopts one emergency phrase that every voice-capable device honors, whether or not the user set one up, and an open interface so safety apps can stop playback and confirm the device complied. Stop always wins over any other command.

Patents

Patents will not be the barrier

Some mechanisms here are covered by U.S. Patent No. 12,347,301 and by provisional patent applications. A provisional is a one-year placeholder no examiner has reviewed, and many of those claims may never become a full application.

The cautionary tale is SawStop. A table saw that stops its blade on contact with skin went on sale in 2004, after manufacturers turned down its inventor's licensing offers. Manufacturers later cited its patents in fighting a federal safety standard, and more than 20 years on that standard is still not in force, while about 30,000 table-saw injuries a year needed medical treatment, about 4,000 of them amputations. A patent thicket in this field could let hundreds of thousands of preventable assaults happen nationally.

The better model is the three-point seatbelt. Nils Bohlin designed it at Volvo, which patented it in 1959 and then left the design open for every carmaker to use. Some of the claims behind this work, together with a few other inventors' claims in this field, may matter as much for bedroom and intimate safety as that patent did for cars. That is why they must not become a toll booth.

Another inventor holds a related patent, U.S. Patent No. 12,512,094 (opens in new tab), on scoring consent from speech and sensor data. In my view its claims don't reach the baseline proposed here, since recognizing one fixed safe phrase computes no consent score, and I've prepared a request for the Patent Office to reexamine it. That is my position, not a legal determination; the State should get its own review.

  • Anything here that turns out to be my invention will be licensed on low-cost, fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory (FRAND) terms, to industry, institutions and other innovators alike.
  • Where price would otherwise block adoption, by industry or by a trial community such as Cornell, the SUNY system or the State of New York, I'll go lower: roughly the cost of the patent filings plus two years of forgone salary, or much less.
  • No proposal here will go unadopted because of a licensing price.

Sexual assault costs the United States about $3.1 trillion over survivors' lifetimes (2014 dollars; Peterson and colleagues, 2017) and makes a woman six times more likely to attempt suicide (Davidson and colleagues, 1996). Fair licensing should let me build a team and fund further experiments against harms like these. When those goals conflict with adoption, adoption wins.

— David Clayman

About

Who is behind this

Mission Zero NY is about every kind of sexual assault in New York, with a special focus on date and acquaintance rape and other sexual coercion, even in the gray zone. It starts with campuses because they can run the statewide pilot and measure results, but it is written for every New Yorker who dates, students or not, from senior prom to 81 and beyond, and most of its proposals reach them all. It was written by David Clayman, founder and CEO of Lifesaver Labs PBC, a public benefit corporation that builds consent and emergency-response technology, including the Safeword (Safer Yes) consent system and Neighbor 911. Every proposal where Lifesaver Labs could benefit is marked, and Lifesaver Labs would have no role in evaluating any pilot.

The goal is for New York to take this up as its own, carrying MissionZeroNY.org forward as a State-run hub, and then to invite other states through an interstate compact to run the experiment with it, driving rates lower and lower. Legislators, campus leaders, survivors, advocates, researchers and engineers are all welcome to argue with it, improve it and help test it.

Why "zero"

We can't reach zero tomorrow. We can get shockingly close.

We know we can't actually reach zero sexual assaults, not immediately and not in the next few years. But if we put our engineering and privacy-engineering talent to this problem, we can drive the number so low it's shocking, and even more shocking when we ask exactly why we didn't try to do this before.
