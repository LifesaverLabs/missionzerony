What we built instead

Years of engineering, and nothing at the bed

Universities and their engineers have spent decades of effort on projects like the ones below. Many of them taught real skills, and the students who built them aren’t to blame. But a system that funds all of this, year after year, and never a button at the bed is, in the sense I use the word, stupid (opens in new tab): it could have learned and changed long ago, and it chose not to.

What got built

  1. 1982 · Carnegie Mellon

    A Coke machine on the network

    Computer science students wired their department’s Coke machine to the campus computer so they could check from their desks whether it was stocked and whether the bottles were cold (IBM (opens in new tab)). It is often called the first internet-connected appliance. Engineers have known for more than 40 years how to make a device in one room report to people somewhere else.

  2. 1991 · University of Cambridge

    A camera on the coffee pot

    Researchers in the Computer Laboratory pointed a camera at the coffee pot in the Trojan Room so they could see whether it was empty before walking down the hall. In 1993 it went on the web, and it became one of the first webcams.

  3. Decades · nearly every campus

    Blue light emergency phones

    Campuses put emergency phones on poles along paths and in parking lots. Most sexual assaults happen indoors, behind a closed door. See the irony of the blue light.

  4. 1980s–2000s · thousands of colleges

    Registration, billing and administrative software

    Colleges spent at least $5 billion on the first wave of campus management software (EDUCAUSE Review, 2014 (opens in new tab)), and have rebuilt and replaced it many times since.

  5. 2004 onward · Michigan, Indiana, MIT, Stanford, Berkeley

    Sakai, a shared course website system

    Five universities, funded by the Mellon Foundation, built a common system for course websites, even though several of them already ran their own (Sakai (opens in new tab)). Universities have kept developing it for two decades.

  6. 2007–2014 · a consortium of universities

    Kuali Student, a student records system

    A group of universities spent an estimated $30 million to $40 million building their own student information system before handing it to a company in 2014 (e-Literate (opens in new tab)).

  7. Since 1981 · 500+ university teams

    Formula SAE race cars

    Every year, student teams around the world design and build a small race car from scratch. A competitive team puts in thousands of hours a year.

  8. Since 1990 · dozens of university teams

    Solar cars

    Student teams build solar-powered cars to race across North America in the American Solar Challenge and its predecessor, Sunrayce, often over two-year build cycles.

  9. National since 1988 · civil engineering schools

    Concrete canoes

    Civil engineering students design, cast and race canoes made of concrete in a competition run by the American Society of Civil Engineers.

  10. Since 1949 · Purdue and beyond

    Rube Goldberg machines

    Students build deliberately overcomplicated machines that take dozens of steps to do something simple, like zipping a zipper or turning off a light.

  11. Since 1997 · hundreds of teams

    Robot soccer

    University teams build robots that play soccer in RoboCup, which aims to field robots that can beat the human World Cup champions by 2050.

  12. 2015–2019 · university teams worldwide

    Hyperloop pods

    Student teams built test pods for SpaceX’s Hyperloop Pod Competition, a transportation system that has never carried a passenger.

  13. Since 1901 · Cornell

    The Dragon Day dragon

    Every spring, Cornell’s first-year architecture students spend weeks building a giant dragon and parade it across campus. It is a well-loved tradition and a real feat of design. Cornell has never put that kind of collective effort into a bedside safety device for the students who sleep in its dorms.

What none of them built: a labeled button by the bed, or a device that answers to a spoken safeword, so a person in trouble can reach help in seconds. It needs less engineering than a race car and less money than a single release of campus software. Why hasn’t a single university built one?

The time it costs

How much human time sexual assault consumes each year

Every year without prevention systems, the country spends an enormous amount of human time investigating, adjudicating, counseling, treating and living with the aftermath of sexual assault. A rough estimate:

About 18 million person-months a year

That is roughly 1.5 million working years, or about 38,000 working lifetimes of 40 years each, every year. Because the inputs overstate in some ways (see below), a fair range is about 20,000 to 40,000 working lifetimes a year.

What it would take instead. I’m not naive about this. Scaling bedroom safety to every institution is more work than any one team can do, and it isn’t buildable by just 20 engineers. But it may be less than you think. It could be as simple as bulk-ordering a cost-effective prepanic button, and agreeing to bulk-buy bedside smart speakers for any student who wants or needs one, or making one a condition of attendance, the way a bedroom smoke alarm already is.

Much of the core can be built by a small team of engineers and product managers working with the respect and authority of the Association of American Universities, then pushed out to campuses that accept it, in the arms of a randomized controlled experiment. Every college and university would still need local staff time to put it in place fully.

A rough tally: a core team of 20 engineers and product managers for three years is about 720 person-months. A two-year trial across many campuses with 100 staff is about 2,400 more. If every one of the nearly 4,000 American colleges then spent six person-months of local staff time installing and running it, that adds about 24,000. The total, about 27,000 person-months, is still roughly 700 times less than the human time a single year of sexual assault costs, and the aftermath comes due again every year.

Caveats. This converts dollars into the hours of work they represent, so it is a work-equivalent, not a stopwatch count; not all of the cost is people’s time. Each year’s lifetime costs are spread over the survivors’ lives, but with a steady rate of assault the yearly total is about the same. The survey counts victimizations rather than victims and includes threatened and attempted assaults, and the CDC figure is for rape, so the top of the range may overstate. It also leaves out what can’t be priced: the hours survivors spend unable to sleep, study, trust or love the way they did before.
