The question

How do we actually get from 1 in 5 graduating women sexually assaulted to 1 in 50 or less?

What combination or bundle of practices will plausibly get us there?

What infrastructure do we need?

Roughly one in five women report being sexually assaulted by the time they leave college (P-01). No single program will cut that tenfold. Our answer is a bundle, tested honestly:

The bundle

  • Plans A, B and C in every bedroom: training rehearsed until it’s automatic, safeword-sensitive devices, and a prepanic button within reach (see below).
  • Law that names the gray zone and sets clear alcohol lines for consent (P-05, P-12, P-16).
  • Safer parties and bars, with free breath tests at the door and drink-spiking test strips (P-34, P-35, P-36).
  • People who come when called: trained, sober peer responders and a safe place to spend the night (P-42, P-44).

The infrastructure

  • A standard prepanic button in the same spot at every bed, with room-level location for responders (P-43, P-77, P-84).
  • One universal safeword that every phone and smart speaker honors, through an open safety interface (P-76, P-79).
  • Breath sensors that show the reading, whether consent is possible now, and when it will be (P-17).
  • Private check-ins on arrival and the morning after (P-45, P-87).
  • Honest measurement: the same anonymous survey everywhere, and a statewide randomized pilot to learn which mix gets us there fastest (the pilot, P-59).

How much could this have prevented already? See the counterfactual model. Who designs the technology? See the engineering committee in P-116.

A statewide goal for New York as a beacon for the rest of the United States and Territories, and a beacon for the nations that choose to follow

Make the first stop the last one anyone needs.

Mission Zero is a plan to drive every kind of sexual assault in New York toward zero, with a special focus on date and acquaintance rape and other sexual coercion, even in the gray zone. It isn't only about college. That risk follows everyone who dates, from senior prom to a first marriage before or after 30, and beyond it: working adults, people who never went to college, and New Yorkers dating again at 18 or 81. It treats assault the way we treat fire: with prevention built into rooms, phones, parties and law, tested in a statewide trial and measured honestly.

Available now

Three plans, bundled in every bedroom

This isn't a menu to pick from. Everyone should have access to all three, Plan A, Plan B and Plan C, in every bedroom where people are sexually active before marriage, and, given the long history of marital rape, in many marital bedrooms too. Bundled together, they give people several ways to call for a pause or for help, whatever state they're in and whatever their stress response:

  • When they can speak, a safeword works even with hands held or a phone out of reach (Plan B).
  • When they freeze, can't find words, or speaking feels unsafe, a silent press of a button within reach still works (Plan C).
  • When fear, alcohol or the urge to appease makes any signal hard, rehearsed habits do the work: partners trained to ask first and to stop at silence, and reflexes practiced until they're automatic (Plan A), backed by systems that notice when affirmation goes quiet (Plan B).

No single option fits every person or every moment. Fight, flight, freeze and fawn each close off some ways of asking for help, so the bedroom should keep the others open.

Plan A isn't enough on its own. Education and training work best when they're anchored in the guaranteed availability of Plan B or Plan C. Knowing that anyone who feels pressured can call for help in an instant if their wishes aren't respected changes behavior before anything goes wrong. It pushes both partners to take more care and attention to get affirmative, enthusiastic consent right from the start and to keep checking as things go on. That protects everyone's innocence, including the person who would never want to cross a line but might misread a moment.

A

Plan A: Better education and psychomotor training

Teach ask-first norms and rehearse the motions until they're automatic: an ask-first handshake before a first kiss, a clear hand signal for later stages, a universal safeword practiced like stop, drop and roll, and evidence-based resistance and bystander training.

No devices · Low cost · P-19, P-20, P-27

B

Plan B: Privacy-focused, safeword-sensitive systems

Phones, watches and speakers people already own coordinate, coach, referee or alert when someone says the safeword, with anything from minimal to full-strength processing that stays on the device. Nothing is recorded or sent unless someone asks for help.

Devices already owned · Choose a privacy level · P-76, P-79

C

Plan C: Gray-zone and red-zone prepanic buttons

A button in the same spot at every headboard. One press for confusion or a gray-zone moment starts a quiet check-in; a red-zone press calls for help. No microphone, no camera, and pressing is always a choice. Call it a prepanic button or a gray-zone button: it's meant to be pressed before panic sets in, just as much as after.

About $20 a button · Try the button · P-43, P-77

The wrong tree

Barking up the wrong tree

Responsibility doesn’t stop at 100%

Like Social Security. The way colleges handle sexual assault looks a lot like the way Washington handles Social Security’s solvency. Everyone serious knows, or should know, that the current path won’t fix it and that hard choices and new strategies are needed. Year after year, no one with the power to make those choices makes them.

Fear of backlash is the reason. Suppose a college equips its bedrooms differently, with a simple, prearranged way to send an alert, and the information responders need, from a room where someone is in a gray, yellow or red zone and needs help now, or better, while they are still confused and haven’t yet reached panic. Somebody will object: students, parents, admissions offices, sexual conservatives, liberals and libertines, the press, strict privacy advocates, advocates on every side of gender balance, and lawyers on every side of how such records might be used as evidence. So nothing gets built.

Who decides. At most universities, lawyers effectively set the agenda, not morally responsible engineers. It is easier to keep putting all of the responsibility on students than to accept a share of it for the institution’s own engineering choices. Those include the choice not to push industry standards toward smarter bedrooms, with safewords every device recognizes, gray-zone prepanic and panic buttons, and safeword triggers drilled as reflexively as “Stop, Drop and Roll.”

It needs a real test. Tested at institutional scale, these tools would show whether this kind of preventive care can cut sexual assault as sharply as I believe it can. Every mature, responsible person who faces this problem honestly should agree that test is necessary.

A green flag, not a red one. Instead, committees spend their time on odds and ends: how the technology might be used or misused, what its false-alarm rate will be, whether prospective students and parents will worry about campus safety if they see such visible efforts. That gets the signal backward. A campus with prepanic buttons at the bed and private safeword sensors in its rooms should be read as flying a bright green flag. The real red flag, burning bright, is the opposite: no such systems, too little investment in them, a pattern of avoiding them, or a plea of ignorance that they were ever possible.

So I propose a 200% model of responsibility.

  • The first 100% belongs to the people in the room: above all to anyone who chose to cause harm, and never to the person who was harmed. Whatever is finally found to have happened at Chi Phi that night, that first 100% rests with the people who were there, and nothing here takes any of it away.
  • A second 100% sits on top of it, copied rather than subtracted. It is shared among everyone who could have made that room safer and didn’t try hard enough: interior designers and architects, device and software makers, landlords and housing authorities, institutions, and local, state and federal officials. All of them left a problem as old as our species, and as obvious, without the simple technical aids it calls for.

Responsibility bioconcentrates. Within that second 100%, like a toxin moving up a food chain, it collects at the top: in university presidents, trustees and offices of general counsel that don’t invest in inventing, testing and scaling this bundle of preventive care, or anything with equal theoretical or proven effect. Major technology companies that could build these capabilities into the devices already in our bedrooms, and haven’t, share in it too.

Courts may accept an industry-standards defense, and sovereign immunity may shield public institutions from suit. Those are legal answers, not moral ones. I hope courts, trustees and the public will take the 200% model seriously.

My own share. I count myself in that second 100%. Within my own Semmelspan, I carry at least as much of it as any single university with an engineering school that left this problem alone, for fear that the knot of privacy and stigma could never be cut in a way students would welcome.

The problem

The system notices too late

Most sexual assaults are not attacks by strangers. They happen between people who know each other, on dates, after parties, in bedrooms, on and off campus. We respond with rape kits, investigations and hearings. All of it starts after the harm. And reporting is rare enough that one person can harm several others before anyone files a report.

Everyone who dates. Campuses get the headlines, but most people at risk aren't students. The risk of date rape and sexual coercion, including gray-zone pressure, starts for many around senior prom and lasts through every stretch of single life: the twenties after school or instead of it, dating again after a divorce or a death, and new relationships at 50, 60 or 81. A bartender in Buffalo, a nurse on night shifts in the Bronx and a retiree on a dating app in Albany deserve the same protection as a sophomore in a dorm. Whether or not someone can afford college, wants it, or has long since finished it, they deserve a minimal bedroom safety standard.

At the 20% rate at which college women report rape and sexual assault to police, someone must harm about 3 people before any report is more likely than not, and about 10 before one is nearly certain (Bureau of Justice Statistics, 1995–2013). Gray-zone pressure, such as persisting after a no, is reported far less, and no one measures it, so the lower rows below are illustrations.

The design gap

Bedrooms were never designed for the harm that happens in them

A dorm room, a studio apartment or a hotel room comes with a smoke detector, a marked fire exit and often a sprinkler head and a carbon monoxide alarm. It comes with nothing for sexual assault, even though more than half of sexual assaults happen at or near the victim's home (RAINN). Whoever designed these rooms made a design failure: they engineered them for fire and left out the harm so many people who date fear most. The people who design a room's furniture, surfaces and standard devices bear responsibility for what those things could have prevented and didn't. Design failures can be fixed.

The technology isn't new. Building intercoms were patented in 1894, and two-way intercoms were common in apartment buildings by the 1950s. Hospitals have wired call buttons at every bed, and affordable hardwired panic buttons, which could be reframed as prepanic buttons for the gray zone or the red zone, have been ordinary parts of security systems for decades.

That dorms, apartments and other buildings weren't built around this kind of safety was a choice: a choice to neglect one of the greatest safety risks people face from their teens through every later season of dating. Someday we need a historical examination of exactly why bedrooms were never engineered to protect women and men from assaults that go uninterrupted because a person freezes, goes still or fawns.

I've been Semmelweissing this since 2015. Like Ignaz Semmelweis, who showed in the 1840s that handwashing saved mothers' lives and was ignored for years, I've argued on and off since 2015 for building sexual assault safety into bedrooms. Despite my best efforts, the message hasn't changed campus housing standards, let alone the standards for apartments, rentals and hotels where most adults sleep. Perhaps after the uproar over the Cornell case, campus housing authorities, landlords, builders and those who influence them will finally build bedrooms with clear, direct safety features for sexual assault prevention, using modern electronics that are privacy-engineered and worthy of people's trust.

Part of the reason may be fear of how it looks. A college that builds a help button into its bedrooms worries that parents will read it as an admission that its dorms are dangerous. I once met a parent who took one campus's gift of a personal emergency bracelet to every incoming student as a sign that the campus was especially dangerous, when it was really a sign that the campus was especially careful.

The answer is for every campus to move at once. When New Jersey required sprinklers in every dorm after the 2000 Seton Hall fire, no campus looked dangerous for having them. If every New York campus adopts the same housing safety standard at the same time, with unobtrusive, privacy-first technology in the same spot in every bedroom, it stops looking like a confession and becomes a baseline, like a seatbelt in every car. Then parents and visiting high school students will ask why a campus doesn't have it yet.

The same logic holds for landlords, hotels and home builders: when the standard is statewide, renters and guests will start asking why a bedroom doesn't have it. The proposals for this are P-39, P-41, P-43 and P-77.

A device is a chance to de-vice. Every device in a bedroom, from a speaker to a lock to a headboard, is a chance to reduce vice: coercion, pressure and harm. The goal is to diminish vice, not sex. If anything, safe sexuality should grow when people feel protected enough to express themselves and explore within safe bounds, under ground rules both partners understand, in caring and loving relationships.

For every couple. Mission Zero is pro-LGBTQ. Every tool and proposal works the same for partners of any gender or orientation, never requires anyone to reveal their orientation to anyone else, and treats every consenting relationship equally. CDC surveys find that lesbian, gay and bisexual people face sexual violence at higher rates than their straight peers, so they stand to gain the most from rooms built to protect everyone.

  2. 2003–2004

    I serve in first-year student government at the University of Chicago, and miss what mattered most

    Semmelspan: about 23 years

    Read more

    As a first-year member of student government, I did no significant work or advocacy on sexual assault prevention. I didn’t adequately realize how serious, pervasive and preventable the problem was, though I certainly should have. It was then, and always has been, by far the greatest safety threat in any university community in North America, and it should have been my number one priority. Instead I spent time on all-night library access during finals week, the campus meal plan and dozens of other far less important issues.

    That year I also took part in a design review session for the South Campus Residence Hall, as the University was designing it and seeking community acceptance of its design standards. It opened in 2009 with 811 student beds. They asked what first-year students needed in it. I was an idiot: I failed to think about or demand a layer of safety built into the beds, bedside tables or walls for all the women and men who would live there, such as standardized gray-zone “confusion or prepanic” buttons, when I could have. The University could have tested it there. If sexual assault rates in that dorm proved lower, or if the theory was accepted as strong enough on its own (this was the University of Chicago, after all), it could have retrofitted the rest of the housing in and around campus with matching fixtures or fixed devices that balance privacy and safety.

    My entering class, the Class of 2007, had 1,172 first-year students. Applying the University of Chicago’s own 2019 campus climate survey rates, the closest measure available, and with 602 women and 553 men entering as first-time freshmen: about 175 of my classmates were likely sexually assaulted during college, by physical force or while unable to consent, about 80 of them by rape or attempted rape, and about 70 in our first year alone. I did barely anything to help them, or to make sure it would never happen again to the classes that came after us. As a member of student government, I had a duty to protect my peers, and I fell far short of it. I was part of the problem.

    I file a provisional patent application applying the voice safeword to sexual consent

    Semmelspan: 11 years, 308 days

    Read more

    U.S. Provisional Patent Application No. 62/099,456 disclosed per-user voice safewords tied to “audio files that are specific to each students’ voice,” a consent log, and breath-alcohol checks of “the capacity of anyone in the room to consent.” The 2016 publication that claims it, U.S. Pub. No. 2016/0196735, added a hub that tells a distress call like “Help! Help! Help!” apart from ordinary speech and calls a “consent first responder.” As far as I know, it was the first filing to bring the spoken safeword into the intimate encounter.

    I wasn’t the first to think of a word that summons help. Three earlier filings described pieces of it:

    • Traylor (Verizon), filed September 26, 2007. Granted in 2014 as U.S. Patent No. 8,660,519. A phone that listens constantly and acts on a spoken phrase only if it matches the owner’s voice print. Its purpose was finding a lost phone, not personal safety.
    • Wearsafe, provisional filed June 13, 2012. Its specification describes a “safe word” spoken to signal an emergency, with different safe words for different kinds of emergency. It was the earliest of these filings to describe a spoken safeword for summoning help, though the claims I checked don’t recite one.
    • Steven Altman (On Guard), provisional filed September 8, 2014. The first of these to claim it: a phone or smartwatch that listens continuously for a word the user trained, then alerts others. The application names “victims of assault, sexual abuse or medical emergencies.” Its claims also use “safe word” in the opposite sense, as a word that cancels a timed alert. The application was abandoned after a 2016 rejection over Traylor and another reference.

    Altman’s filing and mine overlap most: continuous listening, a word trained to one person’s voice, and an alert to helpers, with sexual assault named as a use. Neither of us could have seen the other’s. Provisional applications are never published, and an application stays confidential for 18 months from its earliest filing date. Altman’s became public on March 10, 2016, and mine in July 2016. We filed four months apart and reached overlapping ideas independently. None of the earlier filings had the parts specific to consent: the consent log, the intoxication check, or pairing two partners.

A different night

How a night could go differently

Here is how a date or party night could unfold with systems that can be built and adopted today. Campuses are the easiest place to test them, but the same layers work for anyone dating, in apartments, hotels and homes anywhere in New York. Each step is a layer; none depends on the person at risk recognizing danger in time.

At the door

A registered party runs breath tests at entry and marks served guests, as bars already do with wristbands. Free drink-test strips sit at every bar. Above a set breath-test line no one can consent, and in a group encounter that bars everyone in the room.

Arriving somewhere unfamiliar

If someone arrives late at night at a residence their phone has never seen, after drinking or with a new date, the phone raises one quiet, private check-in: fine, "check on me," or help. Nothing leaves the phone unless they ask.

Before anything starts

If either partner may have had more than one drink for a woman, or two for a man, each has a duty to know the BAC picture: the instant reading and what it means, whether each can consent right now, and if not, when they will be able to. A breath sensor beside the headboard button shows all three, because blood alcohol keeps rising after the last drink. Without a test, the answer is to wait.

At the first "stop"

A press of the headboard button, or a universal safeword spoken aloud and trained as reflexively as stop, drop and roll, sets three things off within seconds. The phone or speaker, listening on the device for that one word and nothing else, stops the music and plays a calm check-in. A silent alert with the room number goes to a trained, sober responder nearby: a peer on campus, a vetted neighbor or a trusted contact anywhere else. If there's no answer within a minute, campus safety or 911 gets the room-level location too.

Minutes later

A responder knocks: "Welfare check." A knock can end an incident without anyone being arrested, and it arrives while harm can still be stopped. If the person at risk wants to leave, a refuge for the night is assigned in minutes: a lockable safe-sleep room or a vetted host, opened with a time-limited key, never revealed to the person left behind.

The morning after

Every attendee gets one optional, anonymous "how was last night?" message. A survivor can lock a time-stamped account in a private evidence vault, and file a sealed name that unlocks only if someone else names the same person.

This is a guess, not a finding.

  • Her allegations are unproven, and the defendants deny or dispute them. The bar she is said to have been served at denies serving her. The district attorney has said her sworn statement in 2024 differs significantly from the lawsuit.

Author’s note on the lines. My own preference is a single consent line, the same for new and established partners: 0.05 at most, and ideally 0.04 or lower, matching the Federal Aviation Administration’s rule for pilots, who may not fly with a blood alcohol of 0.04 or higher, or within eight hours of a drink (“eight hours bottle to throttle”). The public may not be ready to accept a line that strict, so I propose the compromise shown here: under 0.05 for new partners and under 0.08 for established partners. — David Clayman

Why it matters. Even the most generous assumptions put her at about four times the new-partner line as she left the bar, and still rising. A free reading at the door of Chi Phi or the bar, with a projection of when consent would be possible again (P-17, P-34, P-35), would have given her, her sorority’s sober monitors and anyone she met a clear, shared signal that the night’s answer was already “not tonight.” And because her central allegation is about drugs, a breath test is only one layer. The safeword (Plan B) and the bedside prepanic button (Plan C) are there for what a breath test can’t see.

The hardware already exists. Breath sensors that can take this reading are sold today. The author of this site carries a BACtrack C6 Keychain Breathalyzer on his keychain; it costs about $70 plus tax and sends readings privately to a phone app. In our view, adding the consent lines, and a projection of when each will be crossed, is a couple of days of AI-assisted development and testing away for a company like BACtrack.

Commercial or community versions that handle many more readings, with wrapped, disposable, standard-straw mouthpieces, and still deliver rich infographics wirelessly and privately, are within reach. The engineering committee proposed in P-116 could get this done in Ithaca, or elsewhere in New York, with sufficient legal support, so that no one needs to be unaware of their alcohol level and what it means: for the line BACtrack calls the ZeroLine™, its projection of when a reading returns to 0.00, and for what we propose calling the NewYesLine, the projected time a reading falls under 0.05.

The pilot

The first statewide Mission Zero trial

New York would be the first state to run Mission Zero statewide: a three-year, campus-randomized trial across participating colleges and training programs, testing which combinations of policy, environment and technology cut sexual assault the most per dollar. Each arm adds a layer to the one before, so the state sees what each added step buys. Its size follows what New York chooses to include, and a focused start can be inexpensive.

Campuses are where the trial starts, not where the problem ends. Schools can randomize, survey and measure in ways the wider public can't, so they make the cleanest first test. What works there is meant to reach everyone who dates: the same button, safeword and check-ins in rentals, hotels, homes and dating apps, for New Yorkers who never enrolled or graduated decades ago.

Privacy

A slightly different balance, with hard guarantees

This asks people who date to accept a button above every bed and a phone that listens for one word. We accepted smoke detectors in every bedroom because they do one job and report nothing else. Safety tech for the bedroom has to meet that bar, in law and in code.

  • Detection happens on the device, including reading the affirmative signals of the consent envelope. Nothing is recorded or sent unless the person triggers it.
  • Open standards with several certified vendors, so no single company controls the system.
  • Published data flows and verifiable builds, so anyone can check the software does only what it says.
  • Pressing a button or using any tool is always the person's choice. Declining carries no penalty and no adverse inference.
  • A record of a yes from any tool can never be used as proof of consent.
  • No cameras and no brain-signal data in intimate-safety devices.
  • Records stay in the user's custody, encrypted to keys only they hold.
  • Statistics leave a campus, building or app only as de-identified totals, in groups large enough that no one can be identified.

Already here

Tools on the market or coming soon

Much of a Mission Zero setup can be assembled today from products other companies already sell. Every setup should offer both a touch path and a voice path: voice works when hands are held or a phone is out of reach, and touch works when speaking is unsafe. Mission Zero's standards are open, so any of these can be certified and used.

No new hardware required. Every piece can be off the shelf: phones, watches, smart speakers, programmable buttons and breath sensors that are already sold today. New hardware engineering is the slowest and hardest part of any safety system, harder than software and platform integration, and Mission Zero doesn't depend on it.

The real obstacle. What holds this back is focus: a community spotlight, and intense cooperation among industry and institutions, both collaborative and competitive, aimed at the Mission Zero goal. It takes a willingness to take responsibility for the engineering fixes, and to withstand sometimes wild public criticism and early rejection while public understanding matures, until everyone is comfortable with minimally invasive, privacy-centered "bedroom seatbelt" technology for high-voltage moments across the hot-cold empathy gap.

Why platforms wait. The first-mover problem that keeps one campus from adding bedroom safety features also holds back the biggest platforms. A company like Amazon may not want to be the one to spearhead a universal, freely available bedroom safeword on every Alexa device owned by Prime members, possibly for fear of an unpredictable customer backlash or boycott spilling over onto its core shopping business. No company wants to move alone on this, and no individual inventor or small business can make a platform prioritize options to prevent sexual assault in Prime members' bedrooms, students or not, however clearly the need and the capacity are laid out. That changes when Prime members, institutions, the public and the New York State Assembly ask for it together.

What campuses already do. A few colleges have already put panic buttons in bedrooms, which shows it can be done.

  • Coastal Carolina University (opens in new tab) puts one in each bedroom of the phase-one units at University Place, connected to a campus police dispatcher.
  • Lafayette College (opens in new tab) added them to the common area and each bedroom of its college-owned off-campus houses after armed robberies. A two-second press alerts Public Safety. But the student paper reported (opens in new tab) that the buttons sit by the door, close enough to the light switch that students worried about mixing them up, and that they are meant for security emergencies, not fire or medical ones.
  • Most campuses keep duress buttons at entrances and staffed desks, and the newest wave, like the hundreds Pitt installed in classrooms (opens in new tab), is aimed at active shooters.

No campus has tried it at the bed. I haven’t found any campus that puts a button within reach of the bed, or one built for gray-zone moments rather than intruders. That is the gap Mission Zero would fill.

Hotels already decided this. Lawmakers have concluded that a person alone in a room with a stranger deserves a panic button. New Jersey (opens in new tab), Illinois, Seattle and New York City now require hotels to give one to housekeepers who work alone in guest rooms. The hotel industry’s own 5-Star Promise (opens in new tab) has put safety devices in thousands of properties. Hospital and nursing-home beds come with a call button at the bedside as a matter of course.

The student bedroom, where most campus sexual assaults happen, is the one place no one has tried it.

The tools

Built for the moments outside the session

Most safety tools wait for a person to recognize danger and act. These are designed for the moments where that fails: before a decision is made in the heat of the moment, at the point of exit, during a frozen dispute, and in the bedroom itself. Each is patent pending and will be licensed on low-cost, fair terms so that patents never block adoption (see the pledge).

In the bedroom, by choice · Patent pending

An affirmative consent envelope

For partners who opt in, often early in a relationship, music plays only while each person keeps giving a simple sign that things are going well: a tap, a squeeze, or a word they enrolled. If the signs stop, the music fades and a private check-in follows. Consent becomes something the bedroom helps people check on, instead of something assumed to be on autopilot.

That matters because some of the most common reactions to pushed boundaries are quietness and tonic immobility, the involuntary freeze many survivors describe. The wish to stop often forms long before a person says so, and then they may say it softly. A fading song notices the silence. This asks for more trust than the guard state, because devices have to read affirmative, enthusiastic signals, so that processing stays on the device and nothing is recorded or sent. Body signals never count as a yes, either partner can privately drop the envelope as trust grows, and the safe phrase stays armed throughout.

In the bedroom · Patent pending

A guard state armed by an ordinary cue

A song, a routine voice request or a normal device command quietly arms the room's devices, with no visible change. They listen on the device, without a wake word, only for a safe phrase, and keep no other audio. A safe phrase starts a private check-in, then an alarm, then a call for help, each only if there's no answer. False alarms are filtered before anyone calls 911, and any request for emergency help carries a confidence level.

On arrival · Patent pending

A late-night arrival check-in

Apple's and Google's safety check-ins confirm you got home and treat arrival as the end of risk. This one is raised by arrival itself: late at night, at an unfamiliar residence, in a risky context. It's private, dismissible in one gesture, and has a duress answer that looks like an ordinary dismissal.

At the point of exit · Patent pending

A refuge for the night

Someone who wants to leave a partner's or host's room at 2 a.m. often has nowhere safe to go. A discreet request, by button, check-in answer or phrase, gets screened for medical risk first, then matched to a safe-sleep room or a vetted host with no possibility of romantic interest, and opened with a time-limited credential.

After a report · Patent pending

A held-apology and clarification channel

A no-contact order protects the complainant but freezes every misunderstanding in place. Through an ombudsperson, or any office entrusted with the role, a restrained party can submit an apology held for the protected party to open only if they choose, and ask closed, structured questions about the investigator's understanding. Each side can verify what was transmitted, changed or withheld, without relying on the intermediary's word.

During a no-contact order · Patent pending

Distance without surveillance

Phones that hold a shared secret can tell when the two parties are about to cross paths, by Bluetooth or private location matching, and gently suggest a different route, venue or time. Neither learns where the other is. Class and work calendars forecast conflicts early, and both can periodically ask for more distance or less.

Everywhere · Patent pending

One universal safeword

New York adopts one emergency phrase that every voice-capable device honors, whether or not the user set one up, and an open interface so safety apps can stop playback and confirm the device complied. Stop always wins over any other command.

More than assault. The same preventive thinking can head off other bedroom harms, and I’d like to talk about these too:

  • Unwanted pregnancy. Multilayer birth control built on a “five nines” model, 99.999%, the five-star reliability engineers expect of critical systems: a joint commitment by both partners to stack methods so that no single failure leads to a pregnancy neither of them chose.
  • STI testing students actually do. How to make the step from a Cornell Yes agreement to real assurance, actually getting tested and sharing results, so comfortable and ordinary that students take it.

Some of this is easier to show than to read. Many of the arguments and patterns I’d propose don’t fit easily on a website, and some are far easier to demonstrate in person. The nonverbal library is one: simple signals, like the ask-first “Firm Shake” (P-19) and the “New York Yes” hand clasp (P-20), that let partners say yes, slow down or stop, and show clearly how strongly they mean it. I hope Cornell will adopt one, find it successful and sticky at promoting enthusiastic, affirmative, clear consent, and make it the Cornell Yes, and then the New York Yes.

I’m not the best speaker, but I’ve had a long time to pull at this thread. I have some unusual opinions. Not all of them are politically correct, and some of them should get out into the public debate over how Cornell and its peer schools might rebuild a culture of well-informed consent. Damn the politics, full steam ahead. Among them:

  • Why we need to discuss the ape-and-rape spectrum openly and raise awareness of it (P-29).
  • Why we shouldn’t rely on the poorly informed “No means no” campaign alone. Many people freeze, go quiet or fawn instead of saying no, and a single no is far less clear than people think.
    Why a single no is wobbly

    To be clear: I’m not defending anyone who didn’t listen to a no. Anyone who hears a no should stop. My point is that “no” is a very poor codeword to rely on. It has so many variations and so many meanings that depend on context, and a bedroom emergency, when someone needs everything to stop, is not the time to reach for a word that wobbles.

    In ordinary conversation and debate, people say no to each other all the time, and the no often turns out to be open to more discussion, persuasion or argument. Unless a lot of tone, body language and other cues back it up, a single no leaves many questions open:

    • Which of the things happening is it a no to?
    • How long does it last, and how strong is it?
    • What else does it apply to?
    • Is it open to discussion?
    • Is it about arousal, lubrication or preparation, or a total no to that route?
    • Should the other person freeze where they are, or pull away?
    • What do you want instead?

    “No, not until you use more lube” is different from “No, no, no.” A single no is a wobbly statement with many shades of meaning. An overloaded everyday word can’t be counted on to carry all of “Stop completely, withdraw, back off, and don’t ever try again.”

    A double or triple no is much clearer: a global, sharp, sustained, insistent, no-more-discussion no. If the campaign were “Double no or triple no means absolutely no,” I’d be 100% on board. A repeated no is a conserved evolutionary signal with the reliability of a genetic code: easy to script, easy for the body to act on, and something both partners can rehearse and move around together the moment it happens.

    Machines face the same problem. As a software builder focused on preventing sexual assault, I can’t program a listening function that responds in only one way to the word “stop” whenever it comes up in a bedroom. “Don’t stop,” “stop teasing” and “no, keep going” all contain it. Even with audio processed only on the device, in a super-private, short-term buffer that forgets fast, trying to work out exactly why someone said no, and to what, takes context and further definition. The best safewords need far less interpretation of intent. “Mango mango” or “Mayday mayday” is obviously more scriptable, definable and clear as a stop signal: it means one thing, so a device can act on it the moment it hears it.

    None of this makes a single no safe to ignore. Stop always wins, and when a no is unclear, the answer is to stop and ask.

  • Why flinch-testing a first kiss, leaning in to see whether the other person pulls away instead of finding a way to ask out loud, is at the heart of where we go wrong, both on affirmative, enthusiastic consent and on asking about STI testing (P-19).
  • How poorly dating apps are designed for trust and safety follow-through.

That motto is for institutions too. Colleges, campus police chiefs and Title IX offices would prevent far more sexual assault if they took “Damn the politics, full steam ahead” to heart when they find themselves hesitating over a paradigm change like gray-zone, prepanic and panic buttons. Some students, parents and reporters will have concerns, and a few may choose to opt out. Those concerns deserve a respectful hearing and honest answers, but they shouldn’t stall the work, because the safety problem is too serious to wait. Institutions also face a preparedness paradox: visible safety measures can make a campus look more dangerous, and when prevention works, the assaults it stopped never show up, so the effort can look unnecessary. That paradox doesn’t absolve an institution of its duty to act on the precautionary principle. When a harm is this common and this serious, the responsible course is to act now rather than wait until the harm can no longer be denied. Bedroom safety can become part of what it means to join the campus community, starting with each incoming class, much as dorm fire safety is today. Within four years it would reach every class on campus, even if some people object to a randomized controlled trial of sexual assault prevention and bedroom hot-state safety.

Patents

Patents will not be the barrier

Some mechanisms here are covered by U.S. Patent No. 12,347,301 and by provisional patent applications. A provisional is a one-year placeholder no examiner has reviewed, and many of those claims may never become a full application.

The cautionary tale is SawStop. A table saw that stops its blade on contact with skin went on sale in 2004, after manufacturers turned down its inventor's licensing offers. Manufacturers later cited its patents in fighting a federal safety standard, and more than 20 years on that standard is still not in force, while about 30,000 table-saw injuries a year needed medical treatment, about 4,000 of them amputations. A patent thicket in this field could let hundreds of thousands of preventable assaults happen nationally.

The better model is the three-point seatbelt. Nils Bohlin designed it at Volvo, which patented it in 1959 and then left the design open for every carmaker to use. Some of the claims behind this work, together with a few other inventors' claims in this field, may matter as much for bedroom and intimate safety as that patent did for cars. That is why they must not become a toll booth.

Another inventor holds a related patent, U.S. Patent No. 12,512,094 (opens in new tab), on scoring consent from speech and sensor data. In my view its claims don't reach the baseline proposed here, since recognizing one fixed safe phrase computes no consent score, and I've prepared a request for the Patent Office to reexamine it. That is my position, not a legal determination; the State should get its own review.

Issued: U.S. Patent No. 12,347,301

Consent management system over a communications network. Filed February 24, 2025, granted July 1, 2025, to David Morris Clayman. Read the full patent (PDF, opens in new tab).

The patent has 20 claims. Its three independent claims (1, 8 and 15) each cover a system that combines all of these:

  • a consent scoring module that takes two partners’ answers to a pre-interaction checklist, their spoken commands during the encounter and their feedback afterward, and keeps a consent score updated throughout;
  • a consent monitoring module that listens for agreed stop words and go words and responds to them;
  • a standing connection to an emergency dispatch center, kept open so an alert goes out without delay;
  • an emergency response mechanism that, on a stop word or a sign of distress, calls dispatch with the location and what happened;
  • a tamper-resistant record of the checklist, the spoken commands and the feedback, protected by cryptographic hashing.

Claim 8 adds biometric confirmation of each partner’s identity and willing participation, with the dispatch connection run by a third-party service. Claim 15 adds selectable modes: Quick Consent, Healthy Consent and a private practice mode. The other claims add machine-learning refinement of the score, alerts on phones and connected lights, language processing that tells consent commands apart from ordinary talk, emergency-contact preferences, backup alerts by text, email, push or webhook, a hash-chained audit log, and a way for people who reported the same person to find each other anonymously.

A product doesn’t have to match the claim wording exactly to be covered. Equivalents count too, within limits.

More details and equivalents

The doctrine of equivalents. A product can infringe a patent without matching its claim language word for word. If, for each part of a claim, the product has something that does substantially the same job, in substantially the same way, with substantially the same result, it is covered. A system that renames these parts, or swaps one of them for a technology that people in the field would recognize as interchangeable, can still fall within this patent.

In my view, three kinds of substitution fall within this patent as equivalents:

  • A reliable, secure call in place of a standing connection. The claims recite a continuously active connection to an emergency dispatch center. Its job is to get an emergency alert, with location and incident details, to dispatch immediately and dependably. A highly reliable, authenticated and encrypted network call opened at the moment of the emergency does that same job, in substantially the same way (a secure network channel from the consent system to dispatch), with the same result: the alert arrives without meaningful delay. That holds especially when the call has retries and redundant routes, which the patent itself describes as part of the link’s reliability.
  • Processing on the device in place of a server. Privacy was at the heart of this invention from the start. The patent describes an Extreme Privacy mode that keeps processing and storage on the partners’ own devices as much as possible, with direct device-to-device linking. It describes a Private Practice mode that scores locally. And it says the server’s functions may be integrated into the users’ devices or spread across several machines. Running the scoring, monitoring, emergency and record-keeping modules on a phone, watch or smart speaker instead of a web server is therefore an equivalent embodiment. For privacy it is the preferred one: the same functions, computed the same way, with the same results, and less data leaving the device.
  • Scores, grades, bands and labels as one another’s equivalents. The patent’s “dynamic consent score” is not tied to one number format. The patent itself expresses consent as letter grades (an A− and a B+ combine to a B+), as color bands on a connected light (green, yellow, red), and as score ranges. A numeric score, a letter grade, a zone or color band, or a categorical label derived from what the partners say and do, or from combinations of those actions, all do the same job: they track the state of consent through an encounter and drive the system’s response. Each is an equivalent of the others.

The limits of equivalents. The doctrine doesn’t stretch without end:

  • Every part of a claim must be there. Equivalence is judged one claim element at a time. A system that leaves out a whole element, such as the consent score or the tamper-resistant record, is not covered by that claim, however similar it looks overall.
  • An element can’t be read out of a claim. An “equivalent” so broad that it would erase a limitation the claim states doesn’t count. Someone could argue, for example, that an on-demand call erases the word “persistent.” My answer is that the claim’s purpose is immediate, dependable delivery, which a reliable secure call provides.
  • What was given up during examination stays given up. Where a claim is narrowed to get a patent granted, the ground between the original and the narrowed wording generally can’t be reclaimed as an equivalent. This patent’s claims were never narrowed during examination, so no equivalents were given up this way.
  • Equivalents can’t reach prior art. The doctrine can’t extend a claim to cover anything that was already known or obvious before the patent was filed.
  • What is described but not claimed belongs to the public. Alternatives the patent describes as separate options, outside what its claims cover, can’t be pulled back in as equivalents. My position is that device processing isn’t such an alternative. It is the same claimed modules running where the patent says they may run.

Whether a particular product infringes, literally or by equivalents, is ultimately for a court to decide. This is my view, not legal advice.

If you’re unsure whether something you plan to build is covered, ask me. A public-interest license settles the question.

About

Who is behind this

Mission Zero NY is about every kind of sexual assault in New York, with a special focus on date and acquaintance rape and other sexual coercion, even in the gray zone. It starts with campuses because they can run the statewide pilot and measure results, but it is written for every New Yorker who dates, students or not, from senior prom to 81 and beyond, and most of its proposals reach them all. It was written by David Clayman, founder and CEO of Lifesaver Labs PBC, a public benefit corporation that builds consent and emergency-response technology, including the Safeword (Safer Yes) consent system and Neighbor 911. Every proposal where Lifesaver Labs could benefit is marked, and Lifesaver Labs would have no role in evaluating any pilot.

The goal is for New York to take this up as its own, carrying MissionZeroNY.org forward as a State-run hub, and then to invite other states through an interstate compact to run the experiment with it, driving rates lower and lower. Legislators, campus leaders, survivors, advocates, researchers and engineers are all welcome to argue with it, improve it and help test it.

I’m glad to speak at Cornell, or anywhere in New York, about any part of this. See Invite me to speak.

Why "zero"

We can't reach zero tomorrow. We can get shockingly close.

We know we can't actually reach zero sexual assaults, not immediately and not in the next few years. But if we put our engineering and privacy-engineering talent to this problem, we can drive the number so low it's shocking, and even more shocking when we ask exactly why we didn't try to do this before.
